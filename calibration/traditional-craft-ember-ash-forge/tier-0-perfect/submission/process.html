From Bar Stock to Finished Object

Eight verbs between cold and finished.

Heating, drawing, upsetting, bending, punching, welding, quenching, tempering. Every piece that leaves this forge passes through some combination of these eight steps — usually in this order, sometimes not.

Step 01

Heating.

Iron begins to behave when it glows. Coal fire pushes the bar from black to a faint cherry red, then to bright orange — the working window. Read the color, read the heat. The smith does not consult a thermometer; the steel is the thermometer.

Tool of Trade

Coal forge & hand bellows. Bituminous coal coked down to clean fire, drawn through a tuyere by a manually cranked bellows. The same setup used in 1840.

Step 02

Drawing Out.

Lengthening and thinning the stock. The hammer falls on the edge of the anvil; the metal stretches in the direction the hammer drives. Every hammer-strike is also a finish — a flat-faced strike will not look the same as a rounding hammer.

Tool of Trade

3lb cross-pein hammer. Forged by my grandfather in 1958. The original handle, replaced once in 1982. The face still rings true.

Step 03

Upsetting.

The opposite of drawing out — shortening and thickening. The bar is heated locally and struck end-on to gather metal where the design calls for mass: a bolt head, a finial, a bulge in the middle of a poker.

Tool of Trade

Swage block & bickern. A heavy cast block bored with hollows, used to corral the metal as it gathers. The bickern fits in the hardy hole for vertical upsetting.

Step 04

Bending.

Curves are nothing more than controlled failures of straight line. A scroll is begun on the horn of the anvil, refined over a jig, finished by eye. The metal must be hot enough that it bends without cracking but cool enough not to collapse.

Tool of Trade

Scroll forks & bending jig. A pair of round-jawed forks and a series of wooden patterns nailed to a steel plate. Every scroll on a railing matches every other.

Step 05

Punching.

Holes punched hot are stronger than holes drilled cold. The metal flows around the punch rather than being torn by it, leaving fibers intact. Every rivet hole on a heritage gate is punched by hand.

Tool of Trade

Pritchel hole & round punches. The anvil's small round hole receives the punch as it breaks through. A bench of punches ranges from ⅛" to 1".

Step 06

Forge Welding.

Two pieces of iron, brought to a sparking yellow heat, struck together so the surfaces fuse at the molecular level. No filler, no flux beyond borax, no electricity. The hardest skill in the forge and the most rewarding when it takes.

Tool of Trade

Borax flux & clean coal fire. A neutral or slightly reducing fire is essential — too much oxygen and the surfaces scale before they fuse.

Step 07

Quenching.

For high-carbon steels, the work is heated to critical temperature and plunged into oil or water. The molecular structure freezes in its hot state, leaving the metal hard but brittle. This is where blades are born — and sometimes where they shatter.

Tool of Trade

Quench tank with canola oil. Heated to 130°F so the steel doesn't crack. Stirred vertically to prevent vapor jackets forming on the blade.

Step 08

Tempering.

Hard steel is brittle; tempered steel is tough. The quenched blade is heated again — gently this time, in an oven — until the color shifts to straw, then bronze. Hardness is given back to the metal in trade for resilience. The piece is finished.

Tool of Trade

Tempering oven, 350 – 450°F. Three cycles of one hour each. Patience is the last tool any smith uses, and the most important.

Material Spotlight

The steels we trust.

Choosing the right metal is half the design work. Each alloy has its own personality at heat — some forgive, some don't.

Mild Steel

A36 · 1018 hot-rolled bar

The workhorse. Forgiving, weldable, takes a fine finish. Most architectural ironwork and small hardware lives here. Sourced from a New Jersey mill.

High Carbon

1095 · 80CrV2

For blades and edges. Heat-treatable to a hard, fine-grained structure. Demands precision in quenching but rewards it with an edge that holds.

Wrought Iron

Reclaimed, low-carbon

Hand-pulled from demolition sites and antique fences. Fibrous, slag-streaked, slow to forge — but takes a finish nothing modern can match.

Sustainability Note

Old iron finds new life.

Roughly 40% of the iron in our shop is reclaimed. We pull from regional demolition crews, rail yards, and salvaged industrial stock. Where new steel is necessary, it comes from a single mill 90 miles south that uses electric-arc furnaces powered by hydroelectricity from the Hudson. Our coal is anthracite, sourced from a family-owned Pennsylvania mine that has been operating since 1894. We reuse our slag, our scale, and any iron offcut larger than three inches. The fire that does not heat the steel heats the workshop in winter.

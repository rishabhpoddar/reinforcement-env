Anatomy of a Jar.

A jar is a tiny ecosystem. Understanding its layered architecture is the first step toward mastering every ferment in this magazine. Below: the five regions of the modern lacto-jar, illustrated.

Vinegar Pickling
Koji & Mold

1

Weigh Your Vegetables

Strip outer leaves, remove cores, and weigh the prepared vegetable in grams. This number is the foundation of every calculation that follows. Approximation is the enemy of safe lacto-fermentation.

2

Calculate the Salt

Multiply the vegetable weight by your target salt percentage (typically 2.0–2.5% by weight). Use non-iodized salt only — pickling salt, kosher salt, or pure sea salt without anti-caking agents.

Troubleshooting: Why is my kraut slimy?

Slime is usually one of three culprits: too-warm ambient temperature (above 75°F), inadequate salt (below 1.8%), or the wrong cabbage cultivar. Most slime is benign — keep it submerged and ferment another week. It often resolves.

3

Mash & Knead

Sprinkle salt over shredded vegetables and massage aggressively for 8–10 minutes. The salt draws water from the cells, creating the brine that will become your fermentation medium.

“If your wrists aren’t tired by step three, you haven’t kneaded enough. The kraut should weep like a sad cabbage.”

— Brinemaster Sandor, on cabbage
4

Pack the Vessel

Press the salted vegetable into a clean, wide-mouth jar in compressed layers. Pour any liberated brine over the top. Leave at least 2 inches of headspace below the rim.

5

Submerge & Wait

Weight the vegetables down so they remain submerged below the brine. Apply an airlock or burp the jar daily for the first week. Taste at day 7, 14, and 21 — finish when you love it.

Bonus: White scum on top — friend or foe?

Kahm yeast. Harmless but funky-smelling. Skim with a clean spoon. Won’t hurt the ferment beneath, but if left for weeks it can soften the texture.
